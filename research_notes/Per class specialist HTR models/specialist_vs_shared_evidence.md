# Per-domain / per-writer / per-era SPECIALIST HTR models vs a single SHARED model (small-data regime)

Scope note: every number below comes from the cited source, and each one gives the dataset size it was measured on. Lines marked **[snippet]** were seen only in a search-engine summary because the full text could not be fetched, so treat them as lower confidence. Anything under "Inferences" is my reasoning applied to the R.O.A.D. setting and was not measured in any source.

R.O.A.D. context used for judging relevance: 4,093 train lines in total. Class B has 1,043 lines. Class A splits into A1 (1,669), A2 (383) and A3 (1,003). Per-class validation is about 75–310 lines. The current setup fine-tunes Qwen-VL models with LoRA on all the data. Bigger models and more epochs made the score worse.

## Q1. How do collection-/writer-specific models compare with generic / multi-collection models in the HTR literature? (numbers)

### Takeaway
The best-supported result across the literature is a **generic model that is then fine-tuned on the target collection**. It beats the generic model alone, and it beats a specialist trained only on the target data by a much larger margin. A specialist trained from scratch on a few hundred lines is far worse than either. Pure generic models lose to adapted ones by roughly 25–75% relative CER when the target hand or collection was **not** in the generic training data. When the target hand *was* already in the training data, which is the R.O.A.D. case, the extra gain from specializing shrinks to about 6–15% relative.

### Cited Findings
- **ICFHR 2018 READ competition (the canonical "generic → specific" benchmark).** The general training set had 17 documents. Five further documents were targets, at about 25 pages per document, with 15 test pages per target document. Participants submitted results after adapting a pre-trained engine with 0, 1, 4 or 16 target pages. Mean CER over the 5 target documents for the winning system was **25.3% (0 pages) → 12.6% (1) → 8.3% (4) → 5.8% (16 pages)**. For the 2nd-place OSU system it was 31.4 → 17.7 → 13.3 → 9.0; for LITIS 35.3 → 22.5 → 16.9 → 11.3; for PRHLT 32.8 → 22.1 → 17.9 → 13.3. With 16 pages the winner's per-document CER was Konzil 2.8, Schiller 8.2, Ricordi 11.4, Patzig 6.7 and Schwerin 2.3. Ricordi was the only Italian document and stayed worst. — [Yousef et al., Table 8/9 (arXiv 1812.11894)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1812.11894); competition description: [Strauß et al., ICFHR 2018](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/8583807/), [dataset](https://zenodo.org/records/1442182)
- In that benchmark, 1 page is about 19–68 lines, 4 pages about 84–264 lines, and 12 pages about 238–782 lines. So "16 pages" means roughly 300–1,000 lines, which is the same order as one R.O.A.D. class. — [Aradillas et al. Table 4 (arXiv 2012.02544)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.02544)
- **Medieval German manuscripts (Reul et al., DAS 2022).** A "mixed" model was trained on 35 manuscripts, about 12.5k lines of Gothic and Bastarda cursive. Applied out of the box to 4 unseen manuscripts it averaged **6.22% CER**. Fine-tuning on document-specific pages brought that to **3.27% (2 pages), 2.58% (4 pages) and 1.65% (32 pages)**. The authors report that fine-tuning out-of-domain models on unseen scripts was still better than training from scratch. Fine-tuning a model from the *same* script family (in-domain) beat fine-tuning a cross-domain one. — [Reul et al. (arXiv 2201.07661)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2201.07661)
- **German Kurrent general models (Hodel et al., JOHD 2021) [snippet].** Specific models achieved better recognition for the hands they were trained on. However, recognition of "similar but not identical" hands dropped, and "too much training material of the same kind could lead to overfitting". A general model trained on about 5.1M tokens reached **6.53% CER** on a validation set of known hands. Some irregular hands cannot get below 3% CER however much training data is available. — [Hodel et al., JOHD](https://openhumanitiesdata.metajnl.com/articles/10.5334/johd.46)
- **Czech modern handwriting, writer adaptation (Kohút & Hradiš, ICDAR 2023).** The base CTC model was trained on 379k lines from about 4.5k writers. It was adapted to 19 unseen writers with at least 512 lines each. Baseline CER on these writers averaged **5.17%** (SD 4.82%). Fine-tuning on the target writer gave **about 25% relative CER reduction with 16 lines and about 50% with 256 lines**. One line gave about 5%, and 2–8 lines gave 10–20%. — [Kohút & Hradiš 2023 (arXiv 2302.06308)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.06308)
- **Same paper, writer-dependent setting**, where the target writer was already represented in the base training data. This is the setting closest to R.O.A.D. per-class specialization. "Fine-tuning without augmentation was prone to overfitting but still brought a **6% CER reduction** on average. Fine-tuning with [blur/color/geometric/mask] augmentation almost eliminated overfitting and brought a **15%** CER reduction." — [Kohút & Hradiš 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2302.06308)
- **Specialization can hurt when the shared model is already good.** In the same paper, writer 1 had a baseline CER of **2.3%**, which went **up to 2.6% after fine-tuning on 16 lines** and only returned to 2.3% at 64 lines. Writers with high baseline CER gained most, for example writer 4: 12.7 → 8.3 → 6.6%. — [Kohút & Hradiš 2023, Table 3](https://arxiv.org/html/2302.06308)
- **Transkribus practice.** For 20th-century English council registers, a custom model trained **without a base model reached 7.18% CER, versus 5.86% when using the generic "English Eagle" as a base model**, and the base-model run needed fewer epochs. Transkribus warns that a mismatched base, such as a 16th-century Latin model for 19th-century French, "can decrease the accuracy". It also warns that reusing a previous version of your own model as the base, with overlapping ground truth, gives "a high CER". — [Transkribus blog: base models](https://www.transkribus.org/blog/using-base-model-when-training-a-custom-model)
- **Transkribus "super models" [snippet].** Text Titan I cannot be fine-tuned by users or used as a base model. It is positioned as a generic out-of-the-box model and was evaluated on "300 very heterogeneous pages". Users are pointed to custom PyLaia models for collection-specific accuracy. — [Transkribus Super Models help](https://help.transkribus.org/super-models); [Transkribus blog: what are super models](https://www.transkribus.org/blog/what-are-super-models-and-how-do-they-work)
- **MLLMs vs supervised collection models.** A benchmark of zero-shot multimodal LLMs against Transkribus models on English, French, German and Italian, modern and historical, found "no consistent advantage for either approach". The MLLMs were biased toward English. — [Crosilla et al. 2025 (arXiv 2503.15195)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.15195)

### Inferences
- In every source above, a "specialist" that wins is **a generic model plus in-domain fine-tuning**, not a model trained on the specialist's data alone. None of the sources tests the R.O.A.D.-style question: does a model trained on all *four related classes of the same archive* lose to per-class models that start from the same pretrained VLM? The closest analogue is Kohút's *writer-dependent* setting, where the target data was already in the shared training set. That gave only 6% relative CER reduction without augmentation and 15% with it, plus overfitting risk.
- R.O.A.D. classes share language, archive, genre (legal) and transcription conventions, and differ by era and crop style. So the expected benefit of per-class specialization is at the low end, around 0–15% relative CER. It is not the 25–75% seen when adapting to genuinely unseen collections.

### Gaps
- I did not find a paper that directly compares a joint model trained on K related sub-collections of *one* archive with K per-sub-collection fine-tunes of that joint model, at 400–1,700 lines per sub-collection.
- The Hodel et al. full text (the known-hand vs unknown-hand CER tables) could not be fetched; the figures above come from a search snippet.
- Transkribus super-model published CERs per collection type were not retrieved.

## Q2. What is the data threshold below which a class-only specialist underperforms a shared model? How does "shared, then fine-tune on the class" compare with class-only training from scratch and with the shared model alone?

### Takeaway
Training **from scratch** on one class is not viable below about 1,000 lines. At 150–800 lines it gives 17–57% CER, compared with 3–11% for a transferred model. One study reports that CRNNs trained from scratch on fewer than 230 lines did not converge at all. **Shared-then-fine-tune** is consistently at least as good as the shared model once there are about 16–64 target lines, for a CTC model on an unseen writer or document. Compact transformer and recurrence-free recognizers can need 40–80 lines before they beat the base. When the base already covers the target (the writer-dependent case), gains are small and require augmentation plus careful early stopping.

### Cited Findings
- **From scratch vs transfer, historical documents (Aradillas et al.).** Washington with **325 lines**: from scratch 41.1% CER (17.5% with augmentation), transfer from IAM **5.4%**. Parzival with **350 lines**: from scratch 18.2% (12.9% with augmentation), transfer **3.3%**. With 150 lines: Washington 51.6% from scratch vs 9.4% transferred, Parzival 21.9% vs 5.8%. — [Aradillas et al. Table 2–3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.02544)
- **Same paper, ICFHR 2018 target documents, source = ICFHR18-G general set (11,424 lines).** With 12 pages (238–782 lines), class-only training from scratch gave **21.1–45.4% CER** (Konzil 37.1, Schiller 45.4, Ricordi 37.2, Patzig 24.5, Schwerin 21.1). Transfer from the general model gave **3.5–11.2%** (Konzil 4.37, Schiller 9.42, Ricordi 11.2, Patzig 10.6, Schwerin 3.5). The **generic model alone (0 pages)** gave 15.5, 24.6, 39.2, 41.5 and 34.5% CER. So shared + FT beat both the shared model alone and the specialist trained from scratch on every document. Even **1 page (19–68 lines)** of fine-tuning beat 12 pages from scratch on 4 of 5 documents; the exception was Patzig, 27.9% vs 24.5%. — [Aradillas et al. Table 4](https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.02544)
- Aradillas et al. summarize that transfer learning "improves the CER between 10–40% when applied to small training sets, of the order of 300 text lines". They also found that applying data augmentation to the *target* set during fine-tuning did not help or slightly hurt, whereas augmentation on the *source* set helped. — [Aradillas et al., Conclusions](https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.02544). **Conflict:** Kohút & Hradiš 2023 found target-side augmentation during fine-tuning added about 10 percentage points of relative improvement and "almost eliminated overfitting" — [Kohút & Hradiš 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2302.06308)
- **Single-writer historical manuscripts (Pippi et al., ICDAR 2023).** Targets were Washington (526 train lines), Saint Gall (468) and Leopardi (1,303). CRNNs trained from scratch "did not converge" with fewer than 230 lines, so pretraining is required. With a well-chosen pretraining set, fine-tuning on as few as about 5 real lines gave usable models. — [Pippi et al. (arXiv 2305.02593)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.02593)
- **Choice of shared/base data matters a lot (Pippi et al.).** Washington was fine-tuned on 1.25% of its lines (about 6–7 lines). IAM pretraining gave **18.8% CER**, synthetic Washington-styled data 23.9%, ICFHR14 26.1%, LAM 37.2% and ICFHR16 **58.0%**. The best base for each target was the one most similar in appearance *and* language: LAM for Leopardi, ICFHR14 for Washington, Rodrigo for Saint Gall. Multi-author pretraining sets were "more robust" than single-author ones. — [Pippi et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.02593)
- **Transformer seq2seq (autoregressive) models (Kohút & Hradiš, ICDAR 2025).** The base was trained on more than 1M lines, with BASE 33M and LARGE 100M parameter variants, and adapted to 27 writers. Fine-tuning "can reliably start with just 16 lines, yielding a 10% relative improvement in CER, and scale up to 40% with 256 lines". The LARGE variant outperformed BASE or at least did not degrade. — [Kohút & Hradiš 2025 (arXiv 2503.19546)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.19546)
- **Compact recognizers can need more lines before fine-tuning helps (PP-OCRv6, 2026).** For manuscript-specific few-shot adaptation, kraken CRNN improved over the generalized model with 20 lines on all 4 manuscripts. PP-OCRv6 "performs worse after 20 lines" and generally needed **40–80 lines** before improving on its generalized base. On one manuscript it did not improve within the available budget. — [PP-OCRv6 historical (arXiv 2609.20064)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.20064)
- **Corpus-level fine-tuning beats full-corpus from-scratch training.** Fine-tuning a generalized, heterogeneously pretrained model (33-language corpus) on only 25% of CATMuS beat the same architecture trained from scratch on 100% of CATMuS, on all 7 test sets. — [PP-OCRv6 (arXiv 2609.20064)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.20064)
- **Fine-tuning curves and stopping.** In the Czech study, fine-tuning curves were U-shaped, and minimum test CER came *later* than minimum test loss. So selecting the checkpoint by validation loss stops too early. Iterations used were 200–3,000 for 1–256 lines. — [Kohút & Hradiš 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2302.06308). The 2025 paper proposes explicit stopping criteria because stopping is the main risk with small target sets. — [Kohút & Hradiš 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.19546)
- **ICFHR 2018 rule of thumb.** A CER below 10% is "helpful" as a starting transcription. With 16 target pages, most competition entries reached it on only 2 of 5 documents; the winner reached it on 4 of 5. — [Yousef et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1812.11894)

### Inferences
- **Threshold summary (synthesized from the above):**
  - (a) Class-only from scratch: loses to shared at every size up to about 800 lines, usually by 3–10× CER.
  - (b) Shared + class fine-tune vs shared alone, when the class is *unseen* by the shared model: wins from about 16 lines (CTC) or about 40–80 lines (compact non-recurrent models).
  - (c) Shared + class fine-tune vs shared alone, when the class is *already in* the shared training set: small gain (≈6–15% relative CER), overfitting-prone, and can even lose when the shared model's error on that class is already low.
- For R.O.A.D. the relevant comparison is (c), because every class is already in the all-data LoRA training. The classes also have 383–1,669 lines, far above the 16–256-line regime where most gains were measured. **Prediction (inference):** per-class fine-tuning from the shared LoRA adapter should give at most a small gain on A2 (dark parchment, the most visually distinct, 383 lines) and on B (different era and spelling). Gains on A1 and A3 are likely to be about zero or negative. Class-only LoRA from the *base* VLM, without the shared stage, is the variant most likely to lose, because it discards 60–90% of the in-archive data.
- **Validation noise is the practical bottleneck (inference, based on an assumed spread).** With 75–310 validation lines per class, suppose the per-line score has a standard deviation of about 0.15 (an assumption; measure it on your predictions). Then the standard error of the class mean is about 0.017 at n=75 and about 0.009 at n=310. The expected specialization gain is a few percent relative CER. On the competition metric, one character edit per line is worth 0.5/55 ≈ 0.009 points and one word edit 0.5/12 ≈ 0.042. So per-class model selection on small validation splits will often be picking noise. K-fold cross-validation or pooled evaluation would be needed.
- The observation that "bigger models / more epochs score worse" fits the overfitting mechanism reported for writer-dependent fine-tuning without augmentation. Splitting 4,093 lines into four specialists makes each training set smaller and makes that problem worse, not better.

### Gaps
- There is no published threshold specifically for LoRA on 7B–32B VLMs; all measured thresholds are for CRNN, CTC or 33–100M-parameter transformers.
- The Pippi et al. per-size tables (fine-tuning at 50% / 5% / 2.5% / 1.25% vs from scratch) were only partially extracted. Only the 1.25% Washington row and the fewer-than-230-lines non-convergence note are confirmed.

## Q3. Catastrophic forgetting / negative transfer vs positive transfer between related scripts, eras and hands

### Takeaway
Positive transfer dominates when the source and target are similar in appearance and language, and more diverse (multi-writer) shared data generalizes better. Negative transfer and forgetting are documented. They appear when the base was mismatched, when fine-tuning data lacks a language or script the base knew, when the base already performs well, or when the same data is reused. Within one archive and one language, as in R.O.A.D., the literature predicts mostly positive transfer between eras and hands.

### Cited Findings
- **Positive transfer from similarity.** The best pretraining source is the one closest in appearance (paper, ink, stroke width) and language. For example, a single-author Italian 18th-century set (LAM) was the best base for Leopardi (Italian, 1818–1832). Multi-author pretraining gives robustness to new styles. — [Pippi et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.02593)
- **Magnitude of wrong-source penalty.** On about 6 Washington lines, the ICFHR16 base (German, 1470–1805) gave 58.0% CER vs 18.8% for IAM (modern English). The mismatch was mainly language and character distribution. — [Pippi et al., Table 8](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.02593)
- **In-domain script family beats cross-domain.** Fine-tuning a model from the same script family (Gothic or Bastarda) beat cross-script fine-tuning, although cross-script fine-tuning still beat from-scratch training. — [Reul et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2201.07661)
- **Measured forgetting when fine-tuning on a narrower corpus (PP-OCRv6, 2026).** On Czech (CMMHWR task 3), "every generalized model performs best zero-shot and performance tends to deteriorate as more CATMuS data are introduced". CATMuS contains no Czech. The authors call this "forgetting of cross-family knowledge". On Arabic-script MAKHZAN, fine-tuning PP-OCRv6-medium raised macro CER from **18.0% to 18.8%**, with Arabic rising from **16.05% to 23.63%**, while Ottoman Turkish improved from 13.76% to 9.83%. — [PP-OCRv6 (arXiv 2609.20064)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.20064)
- **Specialist loses on neighbouring hands.** Specific Kurrent models "achieved better recognition for known hands" but did worse on "similar but not identical hands", and "too much training material of the same kind" led to overfitting **[snippet]**. — [Hodel et al., JOHD 2021](https://openhumanitiesdata.metajnl.com/articles/10.5334/johd.46)
- **Mismatched or duplicate base models hurt.** Examples are a 16th-century Latin base for 19th-century French, or reusing your own previous model as the base with overlapping ground truth. — [Transkribus blog](https://www.transkribus.org/blog/using-base-model-when-training-a-custom-model)
- **Small-sample specialization of an already-good model can regress**, as with writer 1 above: 2.3% → 2.6% CER after 16 lines. — [Kohút & Hradiš 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2302.06308)
- **Which parameters to adapt.** When the target uses characters or transcription conventions absent from the base's training (for example long-s or historic glyphs), the **decoder must also be fine-tuned**. For native conventions, encoder-only adaptation is often enough. Kohút et al. also report that adding writer-dependent parameters and tuning *only those* was worse than tuning the whole model. Citing Chang et al., they note that LoRA matched full fine-tuning while updating only 0.6% of parameters. With LoRA, tuning the whole model beat tuning the encoder only. — [Kohút & Hradiš 2025 (arXiv 2503.19546)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.19546)
- **Freezing the encoder hurts on small historical sets.** On a TrOCR-base medieval manuscript with 1,666 train lines, full fine-tuning gave 8.0% CER, while freezing 6 encoder layers gave 9.7% (significantly worse). Decoder freezing was tolerated to moderate depth. — [TrOCR medieval ablation (arXiv 2606.24302)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.24302). Aradillas et al. likewise found that freezing early CNN layers degraded performance compared with fine-tuning all layers (as cited in [Kohút & Hradiš 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.19546)).
- **Same writer across eras (LAM).** LAM has 25,823 lines by one author over 60 years, with a dedicated **date-based split** across 6 decades. It is designed to test recognition of the same writer in decades absent from training. — [LAM dataset (arXiv 2208.07682)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2208.07682), [GitHub](https://github.com/aimagelab/LAM). *I did not retrieve its per-decade CER numbers.*

### Inferences
- In R.O.A.D., classes A and B differ by roughly 10–70 years, in the orthography conventions ("heires" vs "heyres", "sd" vs "saide", "ye/yt", more "^" and ":" in B) and in crop and parchment appearance. They share language, archive and document genre. By the similarity criterion (Pippi et al.; Reul et al.), transfer between the classes should be **net positive**. That favours keeping them in one shared training run.
- The main negative-transfer risk in a *shared* model is **convention bleed**: the model writes "heires" on a B line or "saide" on an A line. That is a decoder/LM-side problem. Following the decoder finding (Kohút & Hradiš 2025), a cheap mitigation is to **condition the shared model on the class**, for example with a prompt tag such as "class B / 1670s–1710s hand" derived from the pixel-detectable rule. This avoids splitting the data. It is my inference and not tested in any source.
- The main risk in *specialists* is forgetting general reading skill and overfitting to 383–1,669 lines. The PP-OCRv6 Czech and Arabic results show fine-tuning on a narrower set can raise CER on material the narrower set does not cover.

### Gaps
- There are no measured numbers on era-to-era transfer inside one archive, the exact R.O.A.D. setting. LAM's date-split results would be the nearest proxy and should be checked.
- No source quantifies "convention bleed" (spelling-variant confusion) in shared vs specialist models.

## Q4. Evidence specific to VLM-based OCR (Qwen-VL, Florence, Donut, TrOCR) on domain-specific vs mixed fine-tuning with small data

### Takeaway
Direct evidence is thin. No paper found compares per-domain LoRA adapters with one mixed LoRA on a VLM with 400–1,700 lines per domain. The indirect evidence points one way. The strongest historical VLM (CHURRO) is a **single model trained jointly on 155 corpora**. Fine-tuned VLMs can overfit to the training distribution: Qwen2.5-VL-7B collapsed from synthetic to real data. Heavily optimized TrOCR only *matches* a domain-specialized CRNN at about 1,700 lines. A 2026 study found a small generic-pretrained CTC recognizer beats a Qwen3.5-9B historical VLM when the VLM lacks in-domain data, and loses where the VLM had in-domain data.

### Cited Findings
- **CHURRO (EMNLP 2025).** Qwen2.5-VL-3B was given full supervised fine-tuning (no LoRA) on 97,151 training pages from 155 historical corpora spanning 46 language clusters. Handwritten normalized Levenshtein similarity went from **42.9% (base) to 70.1%**, and printed from 67.8% to 82.3%. It beat Gemini 2.5 Pro by 6.5 points on handwriting. The authors report **no ablations** on data mixing, per-corpus specialists, model size or epochs. — [CHURRO (arXiv 2509.19768)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.19768), [HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.19768)
- **Qwen2.5-VL overfitting to the fine-tuning distribution (Manchu, 2025).** LoRA (r=64) was trained on 60,000 synthetic Manchu word images. Qwen2.5-VL-7B reached 87.5% word accuracy on synthetic data but **43.1% on real historical** data. LLaMA-3.2-11B went from 98.3% to **93.1%**, and a CRNN from 99.8% to 72.5%. The size and robustness of the domain gap depended heavily on the backbone. — [Manchu VLM OCR (arXiv 2507.06761)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.06761)
- **TrOCR on a small historical manuscript (2026).** On I-CT 91 "Cortonese", 13th-century Italian with 1,666 train / 397 val / 454 test lines, the best TrOCR-base configuration reached **8.03% CER**. The **domain-specialized kraken–CATMuS model achieved 7.9%**. Freezing the encoder was harmful, and freezing thresholds transferred unpredictably across datasets. On READ-16, full fine-tuning gave 5.16% CER. — [TrOCR medieval ablation (arXiv 2606.24302)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.24302)
- **Autoregressive transformer adaptation.** A seq2seq transformer (33M/100M) pretrained on more than 1M lines gets about 10% relative CER gain at 16 target lines and about 40% at 256. The larger model is not worse than the smaller. — [Kohút & Hradiš 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.19546)
- **Generic compact model vs a tuned historical VLM (2026).** Medusa is a Qwen3.5-9B line recognizer tuned for historical Latin-script HTR and submitted to CMMHWR. After corpus fine-tuning, PP-OCRv6-medium beat it on Latin (8.70% vs 13.07% CER), Spanish (7.99 vs 11.19) and French (3.36 vs 3.74). **Medusa won on Occitan (5.70 vs 6.62) and Czech (15.73 vs 18.05)**, the languages it had extensive training data for and the compact model lacked. The authors note that VLMs struggle with historical hands and diplomatic transcription norms zero-shot, and note hallucination risk. — [PP-OCRv6 (arXiv 2609.20064)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.20064)
- **Low CER can hide VLM hallucination.** On historical Uruguayan documents, VLMs had lower CER and WER than traditional methods. They still showed "orthographic normalization, spurious content generation, and semantic substitutions", with named-entity errors that barely move CER. — [Hallucinations in VLM OCR (arXiv 2607.24077)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.24077)
- **Zero-shot MLLMs vs supervised collection models.** Results were mixed with no consistent winner, and MLLMs were biased toward English. — [Crosilla et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.15195)
- **Data-mixing to prevent forgetting [snippet, unverified].** A search summary attributes to Qianfan-OCR a 70% domain / 30% general data mix during domain fine-tuning to avoid catastrophic forgetting. I did not verify this in the paper. — [Qianfan-OCR (arXiv 2603.13398)](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.13398v1)

### Inferences
- The "orthographic normalization" failure mode is directly relevant to R.O.A.D.'s "heyres/heires, saide/said, ye/yt" variants. The VLM's language prior pulls toward modern or majority spellings. A *shared* model trained on all classes sees both variants and must use visual cues (or a class tag) to choose. A *specialist* sees one convention consistently. That is the one mechanism by which specialists could plausibly win on word-level edits, especially for class B, which is the minority convention with 1,043 lines. This is an inference to test, not a finding.
- The R.O.A.D. observation that "bigger VLM / more epochs is worse" matches the Manchu Qwen2.5-VL-7B overfitting pattern: the fine-tuned model fits the training distribution and degrades on shifted data. It argues for fewer epochs and lower LoRA rank, not for more fragmentation of the data.
- A low-risk middle path is to train the shared LoRA first, then continue for a short time per class at a lower learning rate, mixing in some other-class data (the forgetting mitigation suggested by the unverified Qianfan snippet, and by the PP-OCRv6 forgetting result). Keep the specialist only where cross-validated gains exceed noise. This mirrors the empirically validated "generic → document-specific" recipe (ICFHR 2018, Reul, Aradillas).

### Gaps
- No study found compares per-domain LoRA adapters with a single mixed LoRA for Qwen-VL, Florence-2 or Donut on historical HTR at 10²–10³ lines per domain.
- No VLM study on the effect of epochs or model size (7B vs 32B) at a few thousand lines was found. CHURRO explicitly did not ablate this.
- Florence-2 and Donut historical-HTR fine-tuning evidence was not found in this search pass.

## Q5. Evidence on per-class / per-domain model selection or ensembles in OCR/HTR competitions

### Takeaway
Competitions with a domain-adaptation track (ICFHR 2018 READ) were won by a **single strong generic model adapted per document**, not by per-class model routing. Ensembling and voting over several models gives consistent gains in historical HTR. I found no Kaggle or Zindi write-up showing per-class specialist routing beating a shared model for HTR.

### Cited Findings
- **ICFHR 2018 READ.** Per-document adaptation of one pretrained engine was the task design itself. The winner, a gated fully-convolutional CTC model with heavy augmentation, beat the runner-up by more than 25% relative CER. Its margin came from a stronger shared model and augmentation, not from document-specific architectures. At 0 adaptation pages it already led (25.3% vs 30.8–35.3% CER for the others). — [Yousef et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1812.11894)
- **Test-time augmentation or ensembles in that competition.** OSU used 37 test-time augmented copies. ParisTech and LITIS used language models (word LM, 9-gram). — [Yousef et al., Table 9](https://arxiv.org/abs/1812.11894)
- **Voting ensemble on historical TrOCR [snippet].** For 16th-century Latin manuscripts, the best single augmented TrOCR reached CER 1.86. A **top-5 voting ensemble reached 1.60**, reported as about 50% relative improvement over the best previously reported TrOCR-base result. — [Transformer HTR of historical manuscripts (arXiv 2508.11499)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.11499)

### Inferences
- For R.O.A.D., an ensemble of several shared models is better supported by the evidence than per-class routing to single specialists. The shared models could vary seeds, epochs or backbones (Qwen2.5-VL-7B, Qwen3-VL-8B), and outputs could be combined by line-level voting or by picking the candidate with minimum expected edit distance. A per-class specialist can be *added as one ensemble member* for its class, which caps the downside if it overfits.
- Routing itself is cheap and reliable in R.O.A.D.: classes are separable from pixels, such as image height above 150 px for B. So routing errors are unlikely to be the limiting factor. The limiting factor is whether any specialist beats the shared model outside validation noise (see Q2).

### Gaps
- No Zindi or Kaggle HTR/OCR competition write-ups on per-class specialists vs shared models were found, including none for the ongoing R.O.A.D. challenge.
- Per-class routing evidence from other OCR competitions (ICDAR 2019–2025 historical tracks, CMMHWR 2026) was not retrieved; only the PP-OCRv6 paper's CMMHWR comparisons were seen.
- Kang et al. (unsupervised writer adaptation), Soullard et al. 2019 (optical and LM writer adaptation) and MetaHTR (Bhunia et al., CVPR 2021) were not fetched in this pass, so their numbers are not included. Kohút & Hradiš 2025 note only that Soullard et al.'s CTC-based fine-tuning experiments on ICFHR 2018 "resulted in worse performance" than the transformer variant — [Kohút & Hradiš 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.19546).
